The Monday Morning Payroll Audit

A few reminders: do not assume unusual means incorrect, focus on items that could create employee impact, compliance concerns, or financial inaccuracies, and document your reasoning.

My Findings

Daniel Lee — 0 hours, on leave, still paid $2,400

High Priority

HR records show a leave of absence beginning July 15, 2026, inside this payroll cycle. The register shows zero regular and zero overtime hours, yet Daniel is still scheduled to receive $2,400 in gross pay. Paid-for-zero-hours is contradictory information on its face — it needs review before payroll is finalized to confirm whether this is intentional (PTO payout, leave pay policy) or a processing error.

Recommended action

Verify leave status and pay eligibility during leave; review the payroll calculation before approval.

Unapproved overtime — Kevin Patel, Michael Reed, Maria Lopez, Emily Davis

Medium Priority

Kevin Patel reported 32 OT hours; ADP notes reference approved weekend coverage for a customer deadline — plausible, but approval and hours should still be confirmed. Michael Reed reported 18 OT hours with no supporting documentation at all. Maria Lopez (6 hrs) and Emily Davis (4 hrs) are smaller amounts, but company policy requires manager approval regardless of size — a small unapproved number is still an unapproved number.

David Nguyen — 76 hours, 4 under a standard period

Low Priority

No notes attached explaining the shortfall. Likely an approved absence or a missed shift, but the variance from the standard 80-hour period should be confirmed to make sure it isn't a missed timecard entry.

Overall Assessment

No widespread payroll issues were identified in this preliminary review — most employees' records look accurate. The primary follow-up items are overtime verification across four employees and one significant discrepancy involving an employee on leave who is scheduled to receive gross pay despite reporting zero hours. Pending confirmation of the items above, the register otherwise appears ready to proceed through standard approval.

Lessons Learned

Unusual activity — large or small — doesn't automatically mean an error, but it does need documentation. Overtime entries should be verified regardless of size to confirm compliance with approval procedures. And discrepancies often only show up when you look at status, hours, and gross pay together — reviewing fields in isolation would have let the Daniel Lee item slip through.

The Overtime Spike

Trend Analysis CA Overtime Rules Root-Cause Investigation

Finance reached out this morning because overtime costs increased significantly compared to the previous payroll. At this point, no one is suggesting the payroll is incorrect — they simply want to understand why overtime costs increased and whether there are any trends we should be aware of.

Please review the attached reports and prepare a short summary explaining what you believe is driving the increase. Remember, our goal is to explain the data, not just describe it.

Total OT cost: $19,860 → $33,280 (+$13,420, +67.6%)

Executive Summary & Recommendations

Of the $13,420 total increase, 97% is concentrated in two departments — 69% in Manufacturing, 28% in Engineering. Manager notes and additional context support a business explanation rather than a payroll error: Manufacturing shipped two large customer orders this pay period, and Engineering logged extra hours supporting final product validation ahead of a launch. Managers confirmed they intentionally approved overtime instead of bringing on temporary staff. At least four employees — three in Manufacturing, one in Engineering — logged more than 30 OT hours each, all with documented approval. The remaining five departments together account for only $470 of the increase, well within normal variation.

Recommendations

  • Validate all overtime hours in Manufacturing and Engineering to fully account for the cost increase.
  • Continue monitoring both departments; consider a formal policy for staffing high-demand periods rather than defaulting to overtime.
  • Cross-check against CA overtime, double-time, and 7th-consecutive-day rules given the volume of hours and weekend work, to confirm continued compliance.

Case Study 3 — Week 5

The Payroll That Doesn't Balance

Reconciliation GL Awareness Financial Analysis

From: Sarah Mitchell, Payroll Manager

Finance is performing month-end close and found a discrepancy between the payroll register and the payroll journal posted to the General Ledger. Before Accounting begins adjusting entries, determine whether the difference is expected or needs further investigation.

Specifically: determine the amount of the variance, identify possible causes, and recommend any additional review or corrective action. We aren't looking for a guess — we need a logical investigation supported by the available information.

Register vs. GL Posting

CategoryRegisterGL PostingVariance
Gross Wages$918,450$918,450$0
Employer Payroll Taxes$70,256$70,256$0
Employer Benefits$46,120$43,720$2,400
Total$1,034,826$1,032,426$2,400

Reconciliation Findings

Wages and taxes tie out exactly — the entire $2,400 variance sits in Employer Benefits. Cross-referencing the benefits enrollment log, three employees (Daniel Lee, Rachel Kim, and Ashley Brown) each added an $800/month employer contribution effective August 1 — the exact same pay period. $800 × 3 = $2,400, matching the variance dollar-for-dollar.

Per the Benefits team, the vendor invoice for these new enrollments hadn't arrived yet at close, and Accounting's practice is to book employer benefit accruals only after the invoice is received — so the GL simply hadn't caught up to payroll yet. No payroll processing errors were reported for this cycle.

Conclusion / Next Steps

The $2,400 variance is fully explained by timing — new benefit enrollments processed in payroll before the corresponding invoice reached Accounting. Recommend Accounting confirm supporting documentation and post the true-up accrual rather than treating this as a payroll error.

Case Study 5 — Week 9

The $18,000 Bonus Question

Supplemental Wages Tax Treatment Employee Communication

From: Sarah Mitchell, Payroll Manager

Finance approved annual performance bonuses for six employees, processed through this week's payroll. HR has already received two questions from employees who believe their bonus was paid incorrectly because net pay came in far below the gross amount in their bonus letter.

Determine whether the differences look like payroll errors or normal withholding, and recommend how Payroll should respond to employee questions like these.

"My bonus was supposed to be $18,000, but I only received $9,663. Something is wrong with my paycheck."
"My $12,000 bonus turned into $6,742. Why did Payroll take more than $5,000?"

Bonus Register — Net Results

EmployeeGross BonusNet Bonus% Received
Maria Lopez$6,000$3,62160.4%
Kevin Patel$8,000$4,82860.4%
Rachel Kim$12,000$6,74256.2%
Michael Reed$5,000$3,01760.3%
Emily Davis$18,000$9,66353.7%
Daniel Lee$7,500$4,52660.3%

Assessment

Bonuses are treated as supplemental wages, so federal and California supplemental withholding, Social Security, and Medicare all apply on top of the gross figure — the bonus letter communicates gross pay, not the expected deposit, which is the root of both complaints. Four of the six employees land within a tight, consistent band (~60.3–60.4% of gross received) that's fully explained by standard withholding — no error.

Rachel Kim and Emily Davis are the outliers, at 56.2% and 53.7% respectively, because their "Other Deductions" line is higher than the rest — $500 and $1,200. Those look like ongoing employee benefit deductions being pulled from the bonus check itself, which raises a real question: should recurring benefit deductions apply to a one-time supplemental wage payment, or should they be excluded? That's a policy question, not an arithmetic error, and it's the one item that needs verification before responding to either employee.

Recommendations

  • Verify whether the additional deductions on Rachel Kim's and Emily Davis's bonus checks are meant to apply to supplemental wages before finalizing a response.
  • Respond to both employees explaining that bonuses are supplemental wages subject to standard withholding — with a full, itemized breakdown so the deduction isn't a mystery.
  • Going forward, set expectations in the bonus letter itself: state the gross amount is subject to withholding, rather than letting employees assume gross equals deposit.

Case Study 6 — Week 11

The Employee Who Says Their Pay Is Wrong

Timekeeping Analysis System Troubleshooting Escalation Judgment

From: Sarah Mitchell, Payroll Manager — Subject: Employee Pay Inquiry, James Carter

James Carter believes he wasn't paid for all hours worked last pay period. He says he worked Monday–Saturday, more than 40 hours, but doesn't see the overtime he expected. Determine whether his paycheck was calculated correctly, identify the cause if it wasn't, and note whether this could affect other employees too.

James Carter's Timecard — Week 2

DayHoursApproved?
Mon–Fri8 each (40 total)Yes
Sat6Yes — approved Monday, after Friday cutoff
Total46 hrs

Paid: 46 regular hours, 0 OT hours, $1,932 gross ($42/hr). Company policy: 1.5× for hours over 40 in a Sun–Sat workweek.

Investigation Summary

James was underpaid. He worked 46 hours in a workweek where overtime applies after 40 — his 6 Saturday hours should have paid at 1.5× ($63/hr), not straight time. Instead, all 46 hours were paid at his regular $42 rate. The cause traces to timing, not a system defect: his Saturday hours were entered after the Friday payroll cutoff, and while his manager approved them Monday morning, payroll had already processed the week using the hours on file at cutoff — so the six hours ran through as straight regular time instead of overtime.

Underpayment: 6 hours × ($63.00 − $42.00) = $126.00.

Recommendations

  • Pay James the $126.00 owed — Payroll should decide between an off-cycle payment or a retro adjustment next cycle.
  • This is confirmed for James, but the root cause (late manager approval after cutoff) isn't employee-specific — search for other late time entries this cycle that could have the same effect.
  • Send managers a reminder of payroll cutoff deadlines if late approvals are a recurring pattern.

The Executive Payroll Review

From: Sarah Mitchell, Payroll Manager — Subject: Monthly Payroll Review for Leadership

You've now worked through several types of payroll investigations — take the lead on the October review for leadership. I don't need every individual employee investigated; identify the issues and trends that matter most from a payroll and business perspective, prioritize them, and provide recommendations for Payroll, HR, and Operations.

Meal Premiums$1,240 → $2,860

Executive Working Papers

Overtime surge in Manufacturing & Engineering

High Priority

+157 OT hours across the two departments, driving the $13,150 OT cost increase. Both managers cite a genuine demand spike — new customer orders and a product launch — not a control failure.

Recommended Action

Evaluate whether this is a temporary spike or a staffing/capacity gap; consider a formal high-demand staffing policy rather than defaulting to overtime every cycle.

Payroll corrections nearly tripled (4 → 11)

High Priority

Payroll IT confirms no system-wide calculation errors — the increase traces to late time submissions, overtime activity, and employee record changes, not a broken process. Nine employees had time submitted after cutoff this cycle, concentrated in the same high-overtime departments; five of those nine had overtime and two had meal-period exceptions.

Recommended Action

Since the system is clean, the fix is procedural: reinforce submission deadlines with managers and build a check for late entries before payroll finalizes, rather than catching it after the fact.

Meal premiums up $1,620; 14 exceptions logged

Medium Priority

8 of 14 exceptions resolved through documentation review, 4 resulted in premiums being paid, 2 remain open. Worth monitoring alongside the overtime trend — both point to the same high-workload departments.

Recommended Action

Reinforce meal-period policy compliance with the affected teams; monitor whether the rate normalizes once the demand spike passes.

Rising employee inquiries to Payroll

Medium Priority

HR reports more employee questions about overtime, meal premiums, and corrections this cycle — plausibly downstream of the same late-submission and correction volume above, and a risk to employee trust in payroll if it continues.

No system-wide payroll calculation errors

Low Priority — Monitor

Payroll IT confirms the configuration and processing are functioning as expected. This rules out a systemic cause and confirms the investigation should stay focused on process and staffing, not the payroll system itself.

Executive Summary — To: Leadership Team

Manufacturing and Engineering both saw a genuine demand increase this cycle — new customer orders and a product launch — which drove a $13,150 rise in overtime costs. Meal premiums more than doubled, plausibly tied to the same higher workload. Payroll corrections rose from 4 to 11, driven mostly by late time submissions concentrated in those same two departments, which in turn likely contributed to the increase in employee questions to Payroll.

Leadership should watch these trends closely if they continue, with higher priority on overtime hours and late-time submissions, and lower priority on employee inquiries and meal premiums, which look like downstream effects rather than root causes.

Payroll

Work with leadership on reducing late-time submissions; reinforce the meal-premium policy; keep monitoring OT, corrections, and meal premiums as a linked trend.

HR

Make sure employees and managers clearly understand policies and deadlines; keep responding to inquiries promptly to preserve trust in payroll.

Operations

Evaluate additional staffing or scheduling support for high-demand periods so overtime isn't the default response to a demand spike.
